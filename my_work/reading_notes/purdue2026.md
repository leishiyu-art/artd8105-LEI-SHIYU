# Stitching Meaning: Practices of Data Textile Creators

## 1 · Why this paper

- **Empirical bridge between visualization and fiber arts:** Directly relevant to *Polyrhythm Loom*'s *weaving-inspired computational grammar* because it systematically studies how real-world textile creators map data into **component structures, substructures, texture, structural layouts, and colorways**.
- **Contrast case for *Polyrhythm Loom*:** Vernacular data textiles (like temperature blankets) are the most widespread form of "data physicalization" in the wild, usually mapping a single linear timeline (e.g., one row per day).*Polyrhythm Loom* can position itself directly against that paradigm by weaving *multi-agent, polyrhythmic*temporalities rather than 1-to-1 linear logs.

## 2 · Summary

- **What it talks about:** An empirical investigation of vernacular "data textiles" (fiber arts that encode information,such as temperature blankets, scarves, jackets, and pillows) based on a survey of **61 creators** and an analyzed corpus of **159 textile artifacts** .
- **Problem & importance:** Tens of thousands of fiber artists already encode data into cloth, yet data visualization and physicalization research has largely ignored this massive community, focusing instead on small-scale academic prototypes.
- **Main contributions & innovation:**
  - A **159-artifact corpus** and an **encoding schema** characterizing how data textile creators map information using component structures, substructures, colorways, and texture.
  - A three-pronged analytical lens covering: (1) *the artifact and its visual/material encoding*, (2) *the creator's process, data-tracking workflow, and motives*, and (3) *the data content*.
  - Insights showing that making data textiles fosters personal growth and deeper data understanding, alongside design opportunities for both creator-facing tools and mainstream visualization (e.g., borrowing textile texture and structural layouts).
- **Advance over prior work:** Moves beyond one-off RtD textile prototypes (e.g., Friske et al., Desjardins & Tihanyi) to map the *vernacular* practices of an existing, large-scale non-academic community for the visualization field.

## 3 · Critical thinking

- **Visual-analytics framing vs. durational ritual:** Characterizing vernacular fiber art primarily through an *"encoding schema"* of component structures, substructures, and colorways risks treating a temperature blanket like a woven bar chart—underplaying how its meaning is produced by **slow, daily temporal attendance** (stitching one row a day across a year) rather than static visual legibility.
- **Maker-only lens leaves viewer reception unexamined:** Surveying 61 creators reveals how making reshapes the *creator's* relationship to data (*"personal growth and data understanding"*), but leaves out how *outside viewers* read these textiles without a key—especially when structural layouts encode personal or idiosyncratic tracking rules.
- **The "tool-building" assumption:** Concluding that visualization researchers should *"support fiber artists with tools formatted to their needs"* assumes that creators want computational or visualization software, overlooking that manual friction—counting stitches, managing yarn, improvising around mistakes—may be central to *why* the practice feels meaningful.

## 4 · Creative thinking

- **Sharpening *Polyrhythm Loom*'s novelty against temperature blankets:** Most vernacular data textiles map *one* external variable onto *one* uniform temporal unit (1 row = 1 day). You can use this as a foil to explain why *Polyrhythm Loom*needs a **relational computational grammar** (*connection, mediation, relational composition*)—because mudflat ecologies involve intersecting, non-aligned temporalities that cannot be reduced to stacked daily stripes.
- **Three-lens self-audit:** Borrow their three-way split (*artifact encoding*, *making workflow*, *data content*) to check that your DIS paper clearly separates (1) how your weave structure encodes ecological rules, (2) how your own design/translation decisions unfolded, and (3) what Cotai mudflat phenomena entered the system.

## 5 · Form — read it as a genre, not just as content

## 6 · Venue signal

- **IEEE TVCG / VIS vs. DIS:** Notice *what* it took to publish data textiles in **TVCG / VIS**: a **159-artifact corpus**, a **61-person survey**, and a formal **encoding schema** aimed at advancing visualization design and tooling.
- **What this means for DIS:** At **DIS**, you do *not* need a 159-artifact taxonomy to legitimize computational weaving.Where VIS asks *"what is the generalizable encoding schema across N creators?"*, DIS asks *"what does this specific, situated RtD inquiry reveal about relationality, materiality, and interpretive limits?"* Knowing that boundary helps you avoid drifting into a VIS-style encoding taxonomy when writing for DIS.

## 7 · Take-home

- **What's new:** A large-scale empirical baseline proving that vernacular data textiles (159 examples, 61 creators) already use *component structures, substructures, texture, and colorways* as sophisticated data representations.
- **Application to *Polyrhythm Loom*:** Cite Purdue et al. in your Related Work to anchor the **vernacular data textile**tradition (linear, single-variable, human-tracked) and contrast it with *Polyrhythm Loom*'s **more-than-human, polyrhythmic** weaving grammar.
- **Good writing sample to imitate?** **Not for your DIS structure**—this is a TVCG corpus/survey paper rather than an RtD paper—though its knack for translating fiber-arts concepts (*substructures, texture, colorways*) into clear HCI/vis vocabulary is very useful to cite.
