# Unmaking-with AI: Tactics for Decentering through Design

## 1 · Why this paper

This TOCHI paper by core More-than-Human Design (MTHD) scholars directly speaks to *Polyrhythm Loom*’s theoretical backbone. It shows how to turn posthumanist theory into concrete RtD tactics (*situating, materializing, enacting*)—and its *Situated Conversations* experiment explicitly unmakes anthropocentric temporalities in favor of ecological/infrastructural time, which sits right next to my Cotai mudflat weaving work.

## 2 · Summary

- What it talks about: The paper bridges *unmaking* and *more-than-human design (MTHD)* by analyzing a 2020 DIS workshop series where participants interviewed commercial voice assistants (Alexa, Siri, Google Home) and role-played speculative non-anthropocentric agents, followed by two small design experiments (*Situated Conversations* and *Unmaking Kites*).
- Main contributions: (1) The concept of **"unmaking-with"**—a posthumanist practice that dismantles humanist design ideals through entanglement with nonhumans; (2) three actionable decentering tactics: **situating**, **materializing**, and **enacting**.
- Problem & importance: Human-centered design struggles to address ecological crises and AI’s distributed agency because it treats technology as neutral tools serving an idealized human user, hiding labor, resource extraction, and nonhuman relations.
- Innovation & benefits: Rather than treating decentering as an unreachable end goal, the authors borrow Lindström & Ståhl’s "double movement" (unmaking unsustainable norms while making space for new relations) and pair it with Barad’s *diffraction* to show how small material/conversational shifts surface hidden infrastructures.
- Advance over prior work: Gives *unmaking* a clearer posthumanist epistemological scope while giving *MTHD* concrete, repeatable design tactics that move beyond critique into prototyping and bodily/material enactment.



## 3 · Critical thinking

- No Limitations section & an unexamined "insider" cohort: There is no Limitations section at all. The workshops ran over Zoom with 9–15 design/HCI researchers (many already working on CAs and MTHD), yet the authors never interrogate how a theory-fluent crowd—or Zoom’s disembodied format—made speculative role-play look easier and more "decentered" than it would be with general publics.
- "Nonhumans" are mostly human ventriloquism: In Activities 1 and 4, humans *impersonate* objects (a lemon, a cushion, a 250-year-old stone frog). Although *unmaking-with* claims to engage nonhumans as active participants, most of the empirical material is human projection rather than material pushback—only *Unmaking Kites* (Fig. 6) lets wind, water, and seeds genuinely act, and it gets barely two paragraphs.
- Bracketing out AI’s actual computational mechanics: In Section 2.1, the authors explicitly put *"engaging with materials involved in AI systems"* outside their scope. Keeping the inquiry at the level of conversational scripts and metaphor sits awkwardly with their conclusion that decentering must be a *"tangible and material move."*



## 4 · Creative thinking

- Sparks: Their *Situated Conversations* Alexa prototype—answering *"What is the temperature?"* with *"It has dropped X degrees from the place where I was assembled"*—is a brilliant example of **unmaking anthropocentric time and scale**. It resonates directly with *Polyrhythm Loom*’s dual temporal modes (shared timeline vs. agent-specific rhythms).
- New application: The three tactics (*situating, materializing, enacting*) could structure a public workshop around Macau’s Cotai mudflat, inviting participants to "interview" mudflat sensors, tidal models, and reclamation infrastructure before interacting with the loom.
- Alternative method: Where Nicenboim et al. rely on verbal role-play to expose hidden entanglements, *Polyrhythm Loom*pushes *materializing* further into **computational grammar**—making conditional connections, interruptions, and uneven rhythms structurally visible in a digital fabric rather than spoken in dialogue.



## 5 · Form — read it as a genre, not just as content

- Length (excl. references): ~15 pages in TOCHI format (~7,500–8,000 words; 20 pages total with 4 pages of references).
- Section proportions: Intro ~10%, Theory/Background (Sec 2) ~17%, Workshop Analysis (Sec 3) ~43%, Emergent Notions & Tactics (Sec 4) ~17%, Discussion ~10%, Conclusion ~3%.
- First contribution claim: Gaps & framing appear in Intro Para 3; explicit two-part contribution roadmap appears in **Para 4** (also in Abstract).
- Contribution sentence (verbatim): *"Building on this, Section 4 introduces two key contributions: first, the concept of 'unmaking-with' as an emergent notion to articulate posthuman unmaking practices, and second, a trio of decentering tactics to enable these practices: situating, materializing, and enacting."*
- Artifact vs. analysis: **Conceptual analysis dominates**—even Sec 3 (43%) spends more space annotating transcripts through *un/making* and *de/centering* than describing technical prototypes.
- Related work: **Bridging + gap-building**—reviews *Unmaking* (2.1) and *MTHD* (2.2) separately, spots what each lacks, and fuses them via *double movement* (2.3).
- Process evidence: Workshop activity diagram, co-created 36-question probe, Zoom/video screenshots, verbatim dialogue fragments, and two follow-up experiment photos—zero quantitative metrics.
- Figures: **6 figures**, 0 tables (workshop flow, questionnaire artifact, Siri probe, Zoom role-play, Voiceflow prototype, field kites). They serve as documentary evidence of speculative/material practice.
- Voice & tense: First-person plural (*"we"*); past tense for recounting the 2020 workshops and prototypes, present tense for theoretical definitions and analytical claims.
- Limitations: **None** as a dedicated section—only brief scope disclaimers in Sec 2.1 and a self-reflexive note in Sec 3.1 about researchers struggling to shed humanist habits.



## 6 · Venue signal

Published in **TOCHI** (and rooted in a **DIS ’20** workshop), this is the gold standard for how the DIS/RtD community evaluates *intermediate-level knowledge*. Notice there are no user-study p-values or usability metrics—instead, it wins on **theoretical precision**, **honest reflection on failure** (admitting participants fell back into "smart wife" clichés in Activity 1), and **distilling transferable tactics** (*situating, materializing, enacting*). For *Polyrhythm Loom* at DIS, your three operations (*connection, mediation, relational composition*) mirror this exact move—just make sure your paper also shows *how* the computational weaving grammar itself enacts material pushback, rather than staying purely metaphorical.

## 7 · Take-home

- Learned & new: Framing design contributions as **tactics** (verbs: *situating, materializing, enacting*) and **double movements** (what is unmade *so that* something else can emerge) makes abstract posthuman theory immediately usable in HCI writing.
- Application: Cite this in *Polyrhythm Loom*’s Related Work to situate *data as relation* within MTHD’s *decentering* lineage,and borrow their honesty in Sec 3.1—reporting moments where your prototype or visitors *resisted* or fell back into conventional timeline thinking will strengthen your DIS paper.
- Good writing sample? **Yes, strongly.** Its pacing (Theory bridge →→ Annotated design cases →→ Distilled intermediate-level concepts/tactics →→ Re-situating in HCI) is an ideal structural template for a DIS RtD paper—just add a clearer Limitations/reflexivity reflection at the end for a conference review cycle.
