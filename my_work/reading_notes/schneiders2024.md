## 2 · Summary

[AGENT DRAFT — rewrite this in your own words. A draft left in place does not count as your summary.]

- **What does this paper talk about?** Cat Royale, an artist-led installation (Blast Theory with HCI/HRI researchers). Three cats and a robot arm share a bespoke enclosure for six hours a day across twelve days. The robot offers play. Humans remain in the loop for safety, welfare, and the audience.
- **What are its main contributions?** Two, stated at the end of the introduction (p. 2). First, world design: the places a robot shares with companion animals and humans, including safe spaces, observation spaces, passive objects the robot can use, and a decoration scheme that works for all of them. Second, multispecies interaction around an autonomous robot: spectating, overseeing, and cleaning up, with humans still needed for welfare and breakdowns.
- **What problem does it address, and why is that problem important?** Robots will meet animals, planned or not, and little is known about designing robots for animals, or about whether they can be trusted with each other (p. 1).
- **What is the innovation in the proposed solution, and what are its key benefits?** The project designs the enclosure, the robot and its toys, and the human roles together, then reflects on twelve days of deployment. The paper treats that whole arrangement as the design, not only the robot.
- **How does it advance the state of the art — what does it do that previous work did not?** [AGENT DRAFT] Related work reviews commercial and digital cat toys, intelligent environments, and a smaller set of robot-world examples (hospital robots, instrumented homes). Cat Royale is positioned as an autonomous robot deploying tangible toys inside a monitored world, with a human moderating decisions. The claim of novelty is the shift from designing the robot to designing the world it operates in.

## 5 · Form — read it as a genre, not just as content

Checked against the supplied PDF on 2026-10-11. CHI is on the venue list in `topic.md`. Counts are approximate because the PDF text extraction splits headers across lines.

- **Length**, excluding references: 16 pages in total. References begin on page 15. Body through the conclusion is about 14 pages, roughly 12,400 words before the references heading.
- **Section proportions** (rough % of the numbered sections, ~12,000 words): Introduction ~6% · Related work ~17% · Approach ~6% · §4 Cat Royale (the design) ~27% · §5 Narrative of the deployment ~19% · Discussion, including limitations ~24% · Conclusion ~2%.
- **Where does the contribution claim first appear?** The abstract (p. 1): designing the technology and its interactions is not sufficient; the world it operates in has to be designed too. The two-part version is at the end of the introduction (p. 2).
- **The contribution sentence, verbatim:** “We distil two key contributions. First, we highlight the importance of world design for robots; of designing key aspects of the places they inhabit alongside companion animals and humans, including providing safe spaces and observation spaces for all parties, enabling robots to flexibly interact with passive objects, and finding a decoration scheme appropriate to all. Second, we reveal how designing a robot to autonomously engage animals requires consideration of inter-species interactions, from spectating, to overseeing, to cleaning up the resulting mess.” (p. 2)
- **Artifact description vs. analysis** — the design account (§4) plus the deployment narrative (§5) take more space than the discussion (§6).
- **What does related work do** — it sorts prior work into cat-play technologies and robot worlds / digital ecologies, and it uses that split to place Cat Royale. It builds a gap as well as listing work.
- **How is process evidenced?** Design documents, meeting notes, observations, autoethnographic field notes, and video. §5 uses timed incidents. Figure 1 shows a seven-point cat stress score on an iPad. Eight figures show the control room, enclosure, design iterations, the three cats, the vision and decision interface, toys, the robot controls, and one failure.
- **Figures:** 8. Photographs and interface screens. They make the enclosure, the cats, and the human control room visible, and they support the world-design claim.
- **First person or passive? Present or past tense?** First person “we”. The installation is told in the past tense. Implications are in the present.
- **Limitations — present? How much space?** Yes. §6.3, short, two points: cats rather than the wider ecology, and no claim that a home would reproduce the installation.
