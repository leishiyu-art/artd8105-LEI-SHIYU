# Temporal Design: Rethinking time in design

## 1 · Why this paper

This is the foundational paper behind **Temporal Design**—the exact lineage *Polyrhythm Loom* builds on when challenging *"common timelines that obscure how different processes become related."* Pschetz and Bastian give me the theoretical justification for moving beyond uniform clock time and "fast vs. slow" binaries, toward a relational view of multiple,coexisting temporalities.

## 2 · Summary

What it talks about: A conceptual and design-theoretical paper that critiques how design treats time as neutral, linear, and uniformly accelerating, and proposes **Temporal Design** as an alternative framework.

- Main contributions: Reframes time as emerging from **relations between cultural, social, economic, and political forces**rather than pace, direction, or subjective user experience, and outlines a four-step Temporal Design process (identifying dominant narratives, challenging them, foregrounding alternatives, and exposing networks of temporalities).
- Problem & importance: Design projects (from productivity apps to "slow technology") tend to accept dominant clock-time narratives as a given external backdrop, flattening complex lived rhythms into a simple *fast vs. slow* binary and masking power asymmetries.
- Innovation & benefits: Shifts the designer’s focus from optimizing speed or slowing users down to **revealing networks of temporalities**—making visible how infrastructures sustain certain temporal norms while marginalizing others.
- Advance over prior work: Moves past *Slow Design* and subjective-time studies in HCI/Design by synthesizing critical time studies (e.g., Bastian, Sharma, Adam) into an actionable, pluralist lens for designing artifacts and systems.

## 3 · Critical thinking

- Still largely socio-cultural rather than more-than-human: In this 2018 formulation, time emerges from *"cultural, social, economic and political forces."* Ecological, biological, and geophysical rhythms are largely absent here—the authors themselves had to extend this framework later (e.g., *Designing Temporal Ecologies*, DRS ’24) to account for multispecies temporalities.
- Diagnostic rather than compositionally generative: The four-step process (*identify, challenge, draw attention to, expose*) is great at **deconstructing** dominant clock-time, but gives very little formal guidance on *how* to computationally or materially compose clashing rhythms so they interact, negotiate, or interrupt each other in a live system.
- "Pluralist multiplicity" can gloss over incompatible collisions: Calling for designs that *"illustrate multiplicity and variety"*risks treating different temporalities as peacefully coexisting layers, under-theorizing moments of **irreconcilable friction or interruption**—like when rapid coastal reclamation or human observation schedules directly disrupt mudflat ecological cycles.

## 4 · Creative thinking

- Sparks: *Polyrhythm Loom*’s two temporal modes—a **shared environmental timeline** vs. **agent-specific generative rhythms**—directly operationalize Pschetz and Bastian’s call to expose dominant timelines alongside alternative networks of temporalities, *while pushing it into a more-than-human computational medium*.
- New application: Their four-step Temporal Design process could serve as an analytical rubric for evaluating *Polyrhythm Loom*’s exhibition encounters—checking whether visitors actually move from noticing a shared timeline (Step 1–2) to recognizing distinct ecological rhythms and their relational network (Step 3–4).
- Alternative method: Instead of merely *illustrating* multiplicity, *Polyrhythm Loom* uses **conditional inter-layer connections** to show what happens when temporalities *clash or break*—turning temporal conflict into a structural property of the digital fabric.

## 5 · Form — read it as a genre, not just as content

- Length & genre: **16 pages** (pp. 169–184) in *Design Studies*—a **theoretical/conceptual design-research article** rather than a lab study or single-system paper.
- First contribution claim (from Abstract):
  > *"We propose Temporal Design as a shift from pace, direction, and subjective experience towards looking at time as emerging out of relations between cultural, social, economic and political forces."*
- Artifact vs. analysis: **Theoretical critique and conceptual synthesis dominate**, using illustrative design cases/artifacts as lenses rather than building a single software system.
- Related work: Synthesizes *Slow Design* / HCI acceleration critiques with **critical time studies** and sociology of time to build a clear conceptual gap.
- Process evidence: Grounded in **critical/conceptual argumentation and illustrative design examples** (no quantitative user-study metrics).
- Voice & tense: First-person plural (*"we argue", "we propose"*); **present tense** throughout for theoretical claims and framework definitions.

## 6 · Venue signal

Published in *Design Studies* (and heavily cited across **DIS**, **DRS**, and **NordiCHI**), this paper shows that high-impact design venues reward **paradigm-reframing vocabulary**—taking an invisible background assumption (*"time is uniform and external"*) and turning it into a **design material** (*"time is designed and can be redesigned"*). For **DIS**, citing Pschetz & Bastian (2018) is practically mandatory for any temporal paper, but DIS reviewers will also expect you to go one step further: show *how* your artifact (*Polyrhythm Loom*) materializes and tests those temporal relations in practice.

## 7 · Take-home

- Learned & new: Moving away from *"fast vs. slow"* (pace) toward **relational temporality** (*how different forces and rhythms co-produce time*) gives me a much sharper argument than just saying "ecological time is slow."
- Application: Use this in *Polyrhythm Loom*’s Introduction and Related Work to anchor why *"common timelines"* obscure ecological relations, and position your computational weaving grammar as a **more-than-human, constructive extension**of Temporal Design.
- Good writing sample? **Yes, for theoretical framing.** Its Abstract and core thesis (*shift from X to Y*) are remarkably crisp—a great model for how to state a conceptual pivot in one memorable sentence.
