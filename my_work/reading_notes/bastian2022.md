# Multi-species, ecological and climate change temporalities: Opening a dialogue with phenology

## 1 · Why this paper

- **Direct productive challenge to *Polyrhythm Loom*'s core metaphor:** The authors explicitly critique musical metaphors like Tsing's *polyphonic assemblages* (*"autonomous melodies intertwine"*, pp. 3, 13–14) for treating species' rhythms as separate tracks—arguing instead that ecological time is **co-constructed in situ** across biotic and abiotic interactions (e.g., *pollinator-bell-heather time*, *snow-water-plant time*).
- **Sharpening your reflection on computational translation:** This gives you exact conceptual leverage for *Polyrhythm Loom*: reflecting on whether contrasting a "shared environmental timeline" with "agent-specific generative rhythms" accidentally treats mudflat actors as autonomous melodies, and how your *relational composition* grammar bridges them.

## 2 · Summary

- **What it talks about:** An interdisciplinary scoping review and conceptual argument urging environmental humanities and social science scholars of time to engage directly with **phenology** (the scientific study of seasonal and life-cycle timing across plants, animals, and insects).
- **Problem & importance:** Climate change is widely framed as a crisis of *time out of joint* (e.g., caribou arriving too late for peak vegetation, birds hatching out of sync with prey), yet humanities work on multispecies temporality relies heavily on geological "deep time" or loose musical metaphors (*polyphony*) while ignoring the empirical science of ecological timing.
- **Main contributions & innovation:**
  - Surveys **Western phenology** (standardized phenophases, citizen science, phenocams, and its temperate/springtime Northern Hemisphere biases) alongside **Indigenous / Traditional Phenological Knowledge (TPK)** (relational seasonal calendars and critiques of extractive data practices and Gregorian clock-time).
  - Reports a scoping review showing **only 20 humanities articles** explicitly engage phenology out of 329 coded studies.
  - Uses a concrete ecological study—Collins et al.'s **International Tundra Experiment (ITEX)**—to show that climate warming causes **divergent timing shifts within a single plant species** (reproductive flowering shifts faster to track pollinators than vegetative leaf-out, which tracks herbivores), proving that time is decentralized, plastic, and co-constituted across species.
- **Advance over prior work:** Moves multispecies temporality beyond static species-level labels (like *"bee time"*) and musical metaphors toward empirically grounded, cross-species and intra-species relational timings.

## 3 · Critical thinking

- **Critiquing Tsing's metaphors while sparing phenology's own computational models:** While the authors critique humanities scholars for relying on loose metaphors and briefly note that Western phenology uses *"futures modelling"* (p. 4) and Gregorian baselines (p. 7), they never interrogate the **statistical and algorithmic assumptions** inside phenology itself (e.g., linear degree-day thresholds, discrete *phenophase* boundaries, or satellite greenness indices) that quietly impose standardized clock-time onto fluid ecological processes.
- **Scoping-review opacity & Anglophone blind spot:** Note 2 (p. 16) mentions coding 329 studies and finding 20 explicit humanities engagements, yet neither the search protocol nor the breakdown is shown—and searching strictly for *"phenology"* in English databases misses vast non-Western ecological-calendar traditions (such as East Asian *24 solar terms / jieqi* or *72 microseasons*) that formalize phenological timing under different names.
- **One-way "dialogue":** Despite promising a two-way dialogue (and briefly asking in the Conclusion how humanities could inform phenology's lack of philosophical rigor or embrace queer temporalities, p. 15), ~90% of the paper focuses on what humanities can *borrow* from ecology rather than testing how ecologists receive humanities critiques.

## 4 · Creative thinking

- **Intra-agent temporal divergence in Cotai:** Borrow the ITEX finding (pp. 12–13) that *different tissues/processes of the same organism* shift at different rates. Rather than assigning one uniform rhythm per mudflat species in *Polyrhythm Loom*, your computational rules (or Discussion reflection) can distinguish *sub-process* timings—e.g., mangrove flowering vs. root sedimentation, or fiddler crab feeding vs. burrowing—responding to distinct environmental cues.
- **Cue-driven vs. clock-driven shared timelines:** Hatfield et al.'s Indigenous phenology critique (p. 7)—that seasons emerge from *relational timings* (first freeze, snow melt) rather than fixed Gregorian dates—gives you a strong argument for anchoring *Polyrhythm Loom*'s shared environmental timeline in **ecological/tidal events** rather than uniform clock intervals.

## 5 · Form — read it as a genre, not just as content

- **Length & proportions:** 24 pages total (pp. 1–24); **~14.5 pages excluding references & endnotes** (199 references on pp. 16–24; endnotes pp. 15–16). Unnumbered sections across the ~14.5 body pages: Abstract/Intro (~3.2 pp.), *Phenology: definitions and understandings* (~2.5 pp.), *Indigenous phenological knowledge* (~1.8 pp.), *Phenology and seasonality in the humanities and social sciences* (~2.2 pp.), *Opportunities for connection and dialogue* (~1.3 pp.), *Taking a closer look at phenology and plant studies* (~2.5 pp.), and *Conclusion + Highlights* (~1.5 pp.).
- **First contribution claim & verbatim sentence:** Appears in the Introduction (p. 2): *"In this article, we propose another avenue for the study of multispecies, ecological and climate change temporalities, which we argue shows much promise for addressing recently-emerged questions about timing and coordination. Specifically we are interested in the potential offered by the field of phenology, the study of life-cycle timing across species, including plants, animals and insects."*
- **Artifact vs. analysis balance:** 100% conceptual essay and scoping review; **zero figures, zero tables, zero design artifacts**.
- **Related work style:** Deep, exhaustive bibliographic synthesis (199 citations) woven into thematic narrative sections, with methodological details pushed into an endnote (Note 2).
- **Voice, tense & limitations:** Collective *"we"* throughout; present tense for theoretical arguments, past tense for reporting the scoping review. Limitations are explicitly stated on p. 3 (acknowledging the scoping exercise is neither systematic nor evaluative) alongside built-in critiques of temperate/Northern bias (p. 6) and TPK extractivism (pp. 7–8).

## 7 · Take-home

- **What's new:** (1) Moving past Tsing's *polyphony* metaphor toward **co-constructed, in-situ relational timings** (*pollinator-bell-heather time*); (2) **intra-species temporal divergence**; and (3) **TPK critiques** of linear Gregorian/clock-time baselines.
- **Application to *Polyrhythm Loom*:** Cite this in your Related Work and Discussion to nuance your *more-than-human temporalities* framing—explicitly reflecting on the tension between modeling "agent-specific rhythms" (which risks looking like Tsing's separate melodies) and weaving **relational composition** where timings co-constitute one another.
- **Good writing sample to imitate?** **Yes for its lucid theoretical prose and its rhetorical pivot** from broad humanities metaphor (*polyphony*) to concrete empirical detail (*ITEX tundra plants*)—just scaled down to DIS size and anchored by your loom artifacts.
