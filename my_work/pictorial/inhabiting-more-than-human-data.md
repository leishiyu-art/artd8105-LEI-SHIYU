# Inhabiting More-than-Human Data: Speaking-for, Listening-with, Weaving-with

**Shiyu Lei / University of Macau** 

## Abstract

Data and environments are often treated as design objects, turning the world into a samplable information source and nonhuman life into measured data subjects. In Design and HCI, more-than-human data is frequently rendered as a stable, portable representation detached from the relations and worlds that produce it [1, 2]. Through a retrospective, first-person annotated portfolio, I revisit three design probes from my practice: Parliament of Things, Listening Sea, and Polyrhythm Loom. Together, they trace a trajectory from *speaking-for* and *listening-with* to *weaving-with*, moving data from a distant object to be looked at toward a felt environment to be perceptibly and practically inhabited. Comparing these probes across four analytical dimensions yields intermediate level design knowledge: three relational modes of engaging with nonhuman data, their respective trade-offs, and a method for writing the entangled designer into the analysis.

**Keywords:** more-than-human design; nonhuman data; data as relation; multisensory; research through design; annotated portfolio.

---

## 1. Introduction

More-than-human data can be understood as a set of relations that are felt and practiced in interaction, rather than as abstract representations detached from the world. Drawing on Actor-Network Theory [3] and non-representational theory [4], this research aligns with recent calls to shift the data paradigm. Current practices often treat data and environments as design objects, turning the world into a samplable information source and nonhuman life into measured data subjects. Consequently, more-than-human data is frequently rendered as a stable, portable representation detached from the conditions that produce it [1].

Giaccardi et al. [2] urge a shift from *data from the world* to *data with the world*, framing organisms, systems, and processes including computational algorithms as data co-producers. Responding to this call through design practice, this research reframes data not just as an object to be represented, but as an environment to be inhabited. Inhabiting data entails designing a spatial, sensory, and temporal condition where people can dwell, perceive through multiple senses, and act, while the data itself reciprocally shapes the human.  

RQ：*When more-than-human data is understood as relation rather than representation, how might interactive systems be designed as data environments that people can perceive and act within?"*

To ground this inquiry, two sub-questions are posed: 

**SQ1** interrogates which configurations of agents, temporalities, and sensory modalities make relational data perceptible and actionable. **SQ2** examines the role of the designer as a data practitioner, and the human-centred cuts that remain even in attempts to decentre the human. 

This research is situated at the intersection of More-Than-Human Design (MtHD) in HCI and critical data practices. MtHD has long challenged human-centred ontologies [5], shifting environmental data from matters of fact to matters of concern [6]. Recent scholarship explores data as co-produced by organisms and instruments [2] and emphasizes multisensory, embodied data physicalization over purely visual charts [1, 7]. Wakkary et al. [8] similarly call for more-than-representational practices, using speculative maps to visualize pluralities. Building on these foundations, this research moves beyond mapping to explore how relational data can be configured into actionable, interactive environments.  
  
Three design probes are brought together through an annotated portfolio [9]. These probes serve as materials for reflection rather than a comprehensive showcase, offering glimpses without claiming to speak for other species. The contribution lies in intermediate-level knowledge articulated in three ways: (1) A cross-probe comparison across four dimensions (agents and relations, time and rhythm, perception, and the designer's position). (2) The articulation of three relational modes (speaking-for, listening-with, weaving-with) and their trade-offs. These are not a linear progression, but configurations for other designers to adapt. (3) A demonstration of a first-person, retrospective method that writes the entangled designer into the analysis.

## 2. Approach: A Retrospective Annotated Portfolio

This research is methodologically grounded in **Research through Design (RtD)**, recognizing that design knowledge is generated through the process of making and its material consequences [10, 11]. Rather than presenting isolated artifacts, this research utilizes a **retrospective annotated portfolio** [9, 12]. By placing three distinct design probes side-by-side and annotating them through shared dimensions, the portfolio extracts intermediate-level knowledge [12] that transcends the individual projects.  
  
Crucially, the designer is positioned not as an external observer, but as an entangled actor. In more-than-human design, **design practice itself functions as the interface and the field**. Through first-person reflection, the research acknowledges that every design decision, whether choosing a sensor or setting a threshold, involves an **agential cut** [13]. Each cut determines what is made visible and what is excluded.   
  
To make this inspection rigorous, literature is synthesized into **four analytical lenses** (Figure B). These lenses form an interpretive apparatus to analyze how configurations distribute agency and condition encounters:

**[Agents & Relations: Tracing Co-producers]** This lens interrogates the ontology of data. Moving away from data as a neutral measurement, it applies Actor-Network Theory [3] and relational data physicality [1] to ask: *Who and what participate in generating a trace?* It examines how organisms, sensors, algorithms, and human conventions are assembled, revealing that data is never purely "raw" but always co-produced by an ecosystem of actors.

**[Time & Rhythm: Organizing Attention]** This lens focuses on the temporal politics of the interface. It extends temporal design [14] and considerations of nonhuman temporalities to ask: *Whose time organizes the encounter?* It analyzes how systems either impose human clocks (e.g., real-time feeds) or allow intrinsic, nonhuman rhythms (e.g., tidal cycles or slow degradation) to dictate the pace of interaction. 

**[Perception & Action: Conditioning Encounters]** This lens addresses the phenomenology of the data environment. Grounded in embodied interaction [15] and more-than-representational theory [4], it asks: *How is relational data made perceptible and actionable?* It evaluates how sensory translations (sound, touch, visual weaving) extend beyond the visual chart, and whether these translations genuinely empower people to act within the system.

**[My Position: Exposing the Agential Cut]** This final lens provides a meta-critique. Operationalizing Barad’s [13] *agential cut*, it asks: *Where does the designer intervene?* It makes visible the sensors, classification categories, and thresholds through which the designer makes a trace count. By mapping what is included and excluded, this lens positions the designer not as an invisible author, but as an entangled actor who inevitably makes human-centred cuts. 

Across this portfolio, the design inquiry underwent a deliberate shift. *Parliament of Things* began by authoring a civic position for soil (speaking-for). *Listening Sea* then narrowed the focus to a single acoustic source, questioning what a remote listener could change beyond their own attention (listening-with). Finally, *Polyrhythm Loom* reopened the inquiry to multiple rhythms around the Cotai mudflat, composing how several traces could shape an encounter without adopting a single pace (weaving-with). These turns represent a shifting design question rather than a linear progression. They do not rank the probes; instead, each retains a different form of designer authority and demonstrates a distinct relational mode.   

*Ethical Note: This research involves no human-participant data. The analysis relies solely on my own build logs, sketches, and iterative design variants recorded during the making process.*

## 4. Speaking-for through Parliament of Things

*Giving soil a place in a civic protocol by designing how people could speak on its behalf.*

This probe organized attention through a civic protocol, bringing soil into public deliberation alongside other human and nonhuman agents. The workshop and toolkit—comprising actor cards, maps, and a proposed breathing column—were designed to provide participants with nonhuman perspectives and boost empathy. Drawing on Latour’s [16] *Parliament of Things*, real-time soil data (humidity/CO2) and human breath data were materialized to translate ecological concerns into civic positions.   
  
However, a critical tension remains: speaking-for is still humans speaking on behalf of nonhumans. The human designer acts as a proxy and translator, selecting which agents enter the discussion and authoring their prompts. While the protocol successfully fits slow soil processes into human civic time, it imposes a human negotiate sequence onto nonhuman temporalities. Consequently, the interaction translates a material signal into a public gesture, without establishing actual ecological consent.  
  
***The design insight: Speaking-for makes a nonhuman concern available for collective action through an authored proxy.***

## 5. Listening-with through Listening Sea

*Bringing the real-time sound of a sea into an interior through sound and touch.*


This probe shifted the inquiry from authoring a voice to making time for listening. It proposed bringing Orcasound hydrophone feeds—capturing orca calls, ship noise, and tidal times—into an interior space via sound and a proposed tactile channel. Applying the eco-technical interface approach [7], the design keeps the transmission infrastructure and instrumentation visible, ensuring the listener encounters a mediated acoustic trace rather than an unfiltered animal perspective. 

The tension here lies in distance and reciprocity. A remote listener is positioned as a tuner of source and gain, waiting for an event that may not occur. While sound organizes attention and tactile translation aims to make changes bodily felt, the single data source remains far from the designer's immediate situation. The diagram deliberately leaves the return path to the marine setting unconnected, exposing a critical gap: sensory presence and actionable participation can come apart. 

***The design insight: Listening-with opens a durational encounter, but sensory presence and actionable participation can come apart.***

## 6. Weaving-with through Polyrhythm Loom

*Composing seven rhythms in one field while retaining the rules through which they are brought together.* 

This probe reopened the inquiry to multiple nonhuman rhythms around the Cotai mudflat. Seven distinct agents (grouped by critical-zone spheres) are woven into a shared visual field. In-situ sensors and public data drive the arrangement, with distant earthquakes entering as interruptions. Drawing on temporal design [14], the system experiments with intrinsic time versus a shared human clock.

Crucially, the computational system functions as an active agent rather than a neutral tool. The designer employs AI/algorithmic logic to author the weaving grammar, while real-time data inputs drive the algorithmic generation of the visual pattern. The algorithm makes micro-decisions—such as how a specific tidal value translates into a specific weave pick—acting as a co-creator of the final composition.

A persistent tension is that both the classification categories and the overarching algorithmic rules are entirely authored by the designer. Furthermore, weaving is an anthropomorphized action. The human is positioned as merely one voice among seven, with the virtual treadle acting only as a backup input. However, decentring human input does not erase designer authority; **it merely distributes it across a network of sensors, data streams, and algorithmic processes.**

***The design insight: Weaving-with lets multiple traces shape an encounter through explicit dependencies, but the relational claim depends on distinguishing authored rules from observed ecology.***

## 7. Reading across the probes

Reading across the probes distinguishes naming an agent from giving a trace an effect (see Figure J). While a proxy connects soil to deliberation, an instrument connects sound to listening, and a dependency connects tidal state to a pattern.

Their narrative forms distribute attention differently: taking a turn (Speaking-for), waiting (Listening-with), and following an accumulation (Weaving-with). Crucially, their sensory reach differs from their capacity to change an ecological relation. Perceptual presence and actionable participation require separate design decisions.

Across all three, I determine what enters the encounter. Decentring human input does not erase designer authority; it requires locating it within the system. *Speaking-for, listening-with, and weaving-with are intermediate-level configurations for comparing practice, not stages toward an ethically complete interface.*

## 8. Discussion & Future Work

**Theoretical Reflection: From Representing to Inhabiting.** 

The trajectory across these three probes moves the design of more-than-human data away from representation and toward inhabitation. By treating data as an environment where humans dwell and act alongside nonhumans, this research echoes Anderson and Harrison's [4] call for non-representational theories and Wakkary et al.'s [8] provocation for more-than-representational practices. 

**Methodological Insight: Juxtaposing the Empirical and the Speculative.**

A critical methodological insight emerged from this portfolio: designing with data requires the continuous juxtaposition of the empirical and the speculative. While empirical anchors (such as in-situ sensors, real-time public APIs, and tracked animal flight paths) ground the design in material reality, speculative elements (like proposed tactile feedback or modeled ecological futures) allow us to explore more-than-human pluralities. The empirical keeps the speculation accountable, while the speculative prevents the empirical from becoming merely extractive.

**Design Implications: Tactics for Data Environments.**

Synthesizing the comparative insights, eight design tactics are proposed. These tactics are organized around the dual concepts of *data as relation* and *data as environment*:

*For Data as Relation (Designing the conditions of co-production):*

1. **Expose the cut** [13]: Place the sensor, sampling boundary, or threshold visibly beside its effect. This includes exposing algorithmic logic. For instance, the *Listening Sea* signal path explicitly locates the hydrophone as a mediating instrument.

2. **Name the co-producers** [2]: Credit the organisms, instruments, algorithms, and providers that make a trace possible. The *Polyrhythm Loom* achieves this by explicitly naming the seven nonhuman agents while acknowledging how its AI-driven weaving grammar actively shapes the generated pattern.

3. **Situate traces in place** [17]: Connect a data pattern to the physical location where it was produced and can be verified. The *Loom* anchors its abstract rhythms to the actual Cotai mudflat transect, resisting the placelessness of standard data visualizations.

*For Data as Environment (Conditioning the encounter):*

4. **Let intrinsic time shape the encounter** [14]: Resist the urge to resample nonhuman rhythms into a single human clock. The *Loom* maps characteristic cycles into 24 picks, allowing distinct temporalities to coexist without flattening them.

5. **Translate across senses** [7]: Specify what sound, vibration, or touch makes noticeable, rather than just translating data into visual charts. *Listening Sea* proposes a tactile translation to make abstract acoustic changes bodily felt.

6. **Decentre human input**: Let environmental patterns continue when nobody acts. The *Loom* allows the weaving field to progress continuously without visitor input, reducing the human to one voice among many.

7. **Keep responsiveness open and non-dominant**: Leave absent feedback paths visible. *Listening Sea* deliberately leaves the return path to the marine source unconnected, acknowledging that sensory presence does not automatically grant the human the power to act upon the ecology.

*Across Both:*

8. **Annotate the designer's cuts** [18]: Record the choice, its alternative, and what it excludes. This extends beyond manual design decisions to **computational cuts**. When delegating generative control to AI or algorithms (as in the *Loom*), the algorithm's rules and thresholds must also be made inspectable. By annotating both human and algorithmic choices, the designer remains an accountable actor within the system.

**Tensions and Limitations.**

Several tensions remain unresolved and are acknowledged here. First, classification and mapping are inescapably human acts; even when trying to decentre the human, the designer establishes the categories. Second, "inhabiting" data remains a fundamentally human mode of perception; this research does not claim to speak for or understand the actual lived experience of other species. Finally, methodologically, this portfolio relies on a first-person, retrospective analysis of design materials, rather than empirical participant studies.

**Future Work.**

Future iterations will address these limits. A subsequent study will introduce participant evaluations to examine whether people can distinguish source traces from authored relations, and how they interpret temporal crossings. For the *Polyrhythm Loom*, future work will move toward material fabrication, exploring how nonhuman agents might physically mark or alter the material during an exhibition period.
