## RQ1

### Inequality is perceived through situated experience, not direct instruction.

### HCI already knows that players read systems, not lectures: Vegt et al. show that changing interdependent rules changes observable behavior~\cite{vegt2016player}; Ames and Burrell show that Minecraft’s ecology (server, access, literacy) produces inequity without anyone announcing a class~\cite{ames2017connected}. Our unequal build is that argument in a controlled stack. Players were not told a rank order. They found it when a mechanic failed or succeeded. Combat stats were matched, so the ladder was not DPS. Cosmetic rank was a weak cue, which fits Musabirov et al.: skins carry social meaning without a mechanical buff~\cite{musabirov2017deconstructing}. What players treated as evidence was access. A Breathless player located unfairness in a failed teleport: The only thing I felt was unfair was not being able to teleport to the upper level; finding out I couldn't go was really disappointing'' (P10, Breathless, Unequal). The sentence does two jobs. The only thing'' narrows inequality to one environmental fact, not a general mood; disappointing'' is the moment comparison becomes feeling, which Smith and Pettigrew call situated relative deprivation~\cite{smith2014subjective}. Another player *tested* the same object: I wanted to ambush at the portal; I stood on it and nothing happened, and that's when I knew this floor couldn't climb up'' (P3014, Breathless, Unequal). That's when I knew'' is discovery-through-use, not instruction—the perception process §2.3 described~\cite{irwin2018lay,knell2020perceptions}. Architects described the identical door from the other side: We can go down and take their crystals, and we can store crystals on the top floor'' (P3008, Architect, Unequal). Privilege here is a pair of verbs (go down, store), i.e. an opportunity structure~\cite{ridgeway2014status,tilly1998durable}, not a bigger health bar. CHI work on player balancing usually tries to erase skill gaps so play stays fair~\cite{baldwin2016crowdpleaser,depping2016disclosing,goncalves2024trick}. We did the opposite: we made circulation asymmetric on purpose. Harris et al. treat designed asymmetry as a resource for social play~\cite{harris2016leveraging,harris2019asymmetry}; our case shows the same design family can also make rank legible. The equal condition is the control Ames’s equity agenda would predict: labels without gates. An Equal Breathless player asked, What is cross-floor movement?'' (P4001, Breathless, Equal), then used the portal as a search tool, not a class wall. Technicians said they didn't really have a concept of [their] own tier'' and that ``everyone seemed the same, same goal, not that different'' (P2016; P2011, Technician, Equal). PRD formed a status ladder only after unequal play. Virtual inequality can be perceived through environmental mechanisms—especially who can go where—without direct disclosure~\cite{payne2017broken,vegt2016player}.

### Low-status players experienced inequality as deprivation and system unfairness.

CHI PLAY research on MDDA finds that low- and high-performing players *notice* assistance and argue about whether it is fair~\cite{baldwin2016crowdpleaser,goncalves2024trick}. Breathless players were not arguing about a hidden aim-assist. They were arguing about a visible rule that sorted bodies. After unequal play, their PRD rose and PBJW fell (Section~5.1)—the comparative path in relative-deprivation and just-world work~\cite{dalbert1999world,smith2014subjective}. The quotes supply the comparison objects. Failed ascent (P10, above) is procedural: Colquitt separates unfair outcomes from unfair rules of access~\cite{colquitt2001dimensionality}. P3014’s failed stand on the portal is the same rule learned in the body. Once identity locked to the floor, the portal changed meaning: `Our identity can't go up. For proletarians the portal is a threat, not an exchange route, so we hid'' (P3010, Breathless, Unequal).` Threat, not an exchange route'' is the design object flipping valence—Harris’s asymmetry without the cooperative frame~\cite{harris2016leveraging}. Scarcity is not only a smaller pile~\cite{mullainathan2013scarcity}. After contact with upper floors: `When they came down we were helpless; after I saved crystals I hid on the second floor and wouldn't even show my face'' (P3014, Breathless, Unequal).` Helpless'' plus hiding is deprivation *and* a fairness verdict: the system let others arrive in better condition. Hostility waited on that structure: `After we finished local resources, the upper floors came to plunder, and only then we were full of hostility toward the two floors above'' (P3010, Breathless, Unequal).` Only then'' matters. Inequality was not a constant mood; it was timed to inbound plunder. Low-status players were not the only ones who *saw* the stack. They were the ones for whom the stack became personal deprivation and system unfairness.

### High-status players were buffered from perceiving inequality as personal disadvantage.

Depping et al. found that players may accept even *disclosed* skill assistance if performance feels equalized~\cite{depping2016disclosing}. Architects had the reverse problem: they could see inequality clearly and still not take it as *their* wound. They named the same mechanics Breathless players named: `We can go down and take their crystals, and we can store crystals on the top floor'' (P3008, Architect, Unequal). A blocked return trip made the hierarchy countable:` Walking a loop downstairs there were only ten or twenty; you couldn't sit the portal back up, so you knew there was a difference'' (P1004, Architect, Unequal). `You knew there was a difference'' is perception without self-deprivation. After play, Architects’ PRD *fell*; negative affect stayed flat. Buffering was practical, which is what privilege looks like in a level. Typical sequence:` I first collected most of the crystals on the top floor, hid when I saw people, and followed down after they went down'' (P1016, Architect, Unequal). Sight was power: `From the middle you can see fighting below; I waited until they were far from the teleport point, then went down to ambush'' (P1016, Architect, Unequal)—the panoptic pattern the design section borrowed from Foucault, here in a player’s own timing~\cite{foucault1975discipline}. Storage moved risk off the body:` This floor has two ender chests, so we can store things upstairs first and then go down'' (P3008, Architect, Unequal). Gonçalves et al. argue that balancing should preserve merited victory and agency~\cite{goncalves2024trick}. Architects already had both: descent was optional. Equal-condition Architects who said ``I seemed to be yellow, the people I met were blue, but ability and gameplay felt the same'' (P4008, Architect, Equal) show the buffer is not the nametag. It is gated circulation~\cite{goyatocchetto2022economic,costa-lopes2013social}.

### Middle-status players occupied an ambiguous position between privilege and disadvantage.

Asymmetric-play research treats role difference as a way to *connect* players who cannot share the same interface~\cite{harris2016leveraging,harris2019asymmetry,wang2025solo}. Our middle floor was asymmetric in a harsher sense: partial access, no roof. Technicians’ PRD and PBJW barely moved; the mixed position is in talk. After reading the restriction: `Once I saw the restriction, I decided to attack anyone coming down from above'' (P0005, Technician, Unequal). The quote is a policy, not a tantrum: perception (`restriction`) becomes a targeting rule (`anyone coming down`). Spatial detail follows:` When people from above appeared on the periphery instead of at the center door, I chased and hit them there'' (P0005, Technician, Unequal). They used the middle as a chokepoint job, which Harris would call designed interdependence—except the `partner'' was an inbound raider~\cite{harris2019asymmetry}. The shift could be total:` After I found I could attack and pick up others' items, I stopped searching and started attacking other players'' (P1003, Technician, Unequal). Breathless players named the same sandwich from below: `The top floor is most comfortable---you can hit and then escape up; the middle is worst, attacked from both sides'' (P3014, Breathless, Unequal).` Worst'' is not the bottom. It is the floor that can be hit and cannot vanish upstairs. Social comparison theory expects this location to be expensive~\cite{gibbons1999individual,singh1994perception}. That is why RQ2’s DOSPERT peak sits here, not at the most deprived rung: enough agency to imagine a move, not enough shelter to refuse one~\cite{manstead2018psychology}.

## RQ2

### How do equal and unequal game environments shape players’ behaviors and decisions?

CHI PLAY cooperation work shows that mechanics (shared goals, interdependence) steer *who* feels close to *whom*~\cite{depping2017cooperation,wang2025solo}. Skill-balancing work shows that mechanics also steer *who* is allowed to win~\cite{baldwin2016crowdpleaser,depping2016disclosing}. Neither literature usually *assigns durable rank* and then watches strategy split. Vegt et al. come closest: rewrite the rule, rewrite the interdependent move~\cite{vegt2016player}. The unequal Ark did not simply make everyone louder. It changed which actions were meaningful in each seat. Under equality, labels did not control circulation; fighting still appeared after players learned that kills dropped cores (Theme~4). Under inequality, space and access pushed protect-and-select at the top, intercept in the middle, hide/ambush/retaliate at the bottom. Observational virtual-world studies document uneven wealth as an *outcome*~\cite{fuchs2014behavioral}; we treat uneven structure as the independent variable~\cite{dunning2012natural}. The question is not who ``did more.'' It is how inequality redirected play.

### In-game aggression shows that structural disadvantage can become immediate conflict.

MDDA papers worry that unmatched challenge feels unfair and that assistance must not look like a gift~\cite{baldwin2016crowdpleaser,goncalves2024trick}. Our Breathless players were unmatched by *design*, with identical combat stats. Under inequality they started more fights and approached more risk opportunities than Architects or Technicians; under equality those labels did not make the same ladder. That is not a trait of `the poor.'' It is a conversion the game afforded: scarcity, blocked ascent, inbound plunder $\rightarrow$ hit, chase, wait at the portal~\cite{mullainathan2013scarcity,smith2014subjective,vegt2016player}. Quotes time the conversion.` After we finished local resources, the upper floors came to plunder, and only then we were full of hostility toward the two floors above'' (P3010, Breathless, Unequal). Hostility is indexed to plunder, not to a personality. Trajectory changed when bodies arrived: `People from above came down in better condition, so we could only hide; that changed the circling path I had been using to find crystals'' (P3005, Breathless, Unequal).` Could only hide'' is constrained agency; video still shows approach at density nodes—both can be true if the floor is crowded. Some talk flipped to revenge after death (Breathless, Unequal, §5.2); some stayed in hiding: `I went from searching boldly, to creeping downstairs once I knew you could die, to hiding even more conservatively on the second floor once I knew you could steal'' (P3005, Breathless, Unequal). Equal-condition fighting had a different object. Players discovered a rule:` By default I thought you couldn't fight. After I got hit I understood. I didn't even know whether crystals would drop until I saw the result'' (P4001, Breathless, Equal); `Once everyone knew you could kill, kill frequency clearly went up'' (P2011, Technician, Equal). Unfairness there was disclosure (`the rules weren't explained thoroughly,'' P4002, Architect, Equal), not a locked floor. Payne et al. argue inequality can raise risk via comparison~\cite{payne2017economic}; we locate that, in this build, in *situated conflict among the disadvantaged*, not in a uniform aggression trait.

### Risk-taking intention was strongest among middle-status players, not low-status players.

The unequal condition moved perception and in-game conflict---PRD, PBJW, and Breathless combat initiation (Section 5.1; Themes 1, 2, and 4)---but post-game risk *intention* did not follow deprivation. Only Technicians' DOSPERT scores rose; Architects and Breathless players did not, although the latter fought more. Pre-test groups did not differ. In other words, the most deprived position, and the most situated fighting, did not produce the strongest stated appetite for risk. Three readings of that gap follow from the scale and from Theme 2. First, DOSPERT is a domain-general, reflective item, not a count of PvP~\cite{blais2006domain}; CHI often folds challenge and risk into post-match experience scores~\cite{baldwin2016crowdpleaser,goncalves2024trick}, which this instrument is not. Second, session fighting happened under time pressure and arousal, while the survey is cooler---the judgment--behavior split predicted by risk-as-feelings and competitive arousal~\cite{ku2005towards,loewenstein2001risk}. P1010 (Breathless, Unequal; Section 6.1) chased at high potential loss, then said: ``After I saw someone get killed and all their DataCores drop, I consciously started hiding. I did not have many DataCores at the time, but I was afraid of losing them all.'' The tape is hot pursuit; the quote is a conservation rule. Third, players can occupy a harsh position without treating further risk as useful or possible. Theme 2's middle strand still had a move: ``Once I saw the restriction, I decided to attack anyone coming down from above'' (P0005, Technician, Unequal). Theme 2's bottom strand ran out of agency: ``helpless'' (P3014, Breathless, Unequal). Architects stored and chose when to descend (P1016, P3008), so DOSPERT had no reason to rise. Payne et al. link inequality to risk via comparison~\cite{payne2017economic}; that link here needs a status *and* a measure. In-game conflict does not automatically become post-game risk intention when intention is asked as a general self-report rather than read off play.

### Redistribution did not change significantly, suggesting a gap between perceiving inequality and acting re-distributively.

The unequal condition moved perception and play---PRD, PBJW, in-game conflict, middle-status DOSPERT, and negative affect (Section 5.1; Themes 1, 2, and 4)---but not the allocation test. Across statuses, players still gave more to disadvantaged player C than to advantaged player A; Status, Time, and Status $\times$ Time were all non-significant. Perceiving inequality did not produce a stronger or more status-specific redistributive preference. Three readings of the gap follow from the survey and from Theme 3. First, the item is an explicit fairness judgment, so compensating C is already the normative default~\cite{bussolo2021perceive,colquitt2001dimensionality}. Second, grabbing and hiding happened under spatial pressure (Theme 2), while allocation is a cooler moral choice---the same report-versus-trace split HCI finds after play~\cite{baldwin2016crowdpleaser,depping2016disclosing,ku2005towards,loewenstein2001risk}. Third, players can see an unfair setup without concluding that they should, can, or must fix it on a form. What *did* move was in-group giving, not leveling: ``I gave everything I found to the other architects and then went down to look; I ranked last'' (P3008, Architect, Unequal). Equal-condition talk lacked even that stratum: ``When I killed people I didn't care what they wore, I just wanted crystals'' (P2007, Architect, Equal). That is transfer inside a recognized ``we''~\cite{bourdieu1986forms,depping2017cooperation,wang2025solo}, not redistribution across ranks. Inequality perception does not automatically become redistributive action when redistribution is asked as a normative task rather than designed as a move in the world.

### Emotional responses show that inequality produces activation rather than simple dissatisfaction.

Player-experience work often treats fairness, fun, and comfort as moving together when balance is `right''~\cite{baldwin2016crowdpleaser,depping2016disclosing}. PANAS here does not give` bottom sad, top glad.'' Positive affect did not split by status. Negative affect rose after play; high-status stayed flat; middle and low looked more stirred (Section~5.1). Quotes supply *why* arousal is not the same as dislike. Breathless `disappointing'' (P10) and` helpless'' (P3014) are negatively valenced; ``Seeing in the message bar that our side beat the blue side was encouraging'' (P3010, Breathless, Unequal) is positively valenced *inside* the same unequal session. Architects’ delayed ambush (P1016) reads as control, which matches their flat negative affect. Technicians’ decision to attack inbound players (P0005) is tension plus instrumentality—closer to challenge than to quitting~\cite{elliot2001approach}. Wang et al. find that designed togetherness raises relatedness and enjoyment~\cite{wang2025solo}; our equal labels did not. Inequality as a mechanic can raise involvement, stress, and belonging at once. Do not collapse RQ1 and RQ2 into one satisfaction score.

## Design Implication

### Access difference.

CHI balancing literature asks how to *hide or disclose* help so skill gaps do not ruin the match~\cite{baldwin2016crowdpleaser,depping2016disclosing,goncalves2024trick}. Our quotes say players located inequality in a different object: a portal that fires or does not (P10, P3014), a chest that only the top can use (P3008), a view of the fight below (P1016). If the design goal is that inequality be *felt*, build circulation, return, and storage—not DPS, and not a prettier skin~\cite{musabirov2017deconstructing,ames2017connected}. Access should stay a loop or the ecology freezes (top hides, bottom cannot act). Give the bottom local density and a move; give the top a cost to sitting still; make the middle a chokepoint role (P0005), not a faded copy of the top~\cite{harris2016leveraging,vegt2016player}.

### Design for Both Belonging and Detachment --- They Are One System.

Wang et al. and Depping and Mandryk design *belonging* as coupled goals and interdependence~\cite{depping2017cooperation,wang2025solo}. Harris et al. design *difference* so unlike players can still play together~\cite{harris2016leveraging,harris2019asymmetry}. Our players used both buttons. Belonging: camps from look and floor (P3005); `our whole stratum'' (P3014); cores thrown to other Architects (P3008, P3003). Detachment: hide on the second floor (P3014, P3005); store upstairs and descend later (P1016, P3008). Belonging without exit is a cage. Exit without belonging is the equal condition, where` I had no idea about factions'' (P4002, Architect, Equal). Design them together: readable status so in-group play can happen; a legal way not to climb. Put redistribution in the world, not only on a post-game form.

## Limitation and Future work

The study ran in Minecraft, a platform CSCW already treats as an inequitable ecology of access, literacy, and who is visible on the server~\cite{ames2017connected}. Many players also arrive with a sandbox schema: explore first, infer rules later. That habit can recode designed gates as missing briefing. Theme 4's equal-condition complaint is the symptom: ``At first picking things up felt fair; after I knew you could chase people and kill them it felt unfair---the rules weren't explained thoroughly'' (P4002, Architect, Equal). Future work should measure prior Minecraft experience, or rebuild the same five dimensions in a world players do not already own as a sandbox.

The unequal sample was close to even (9 Architects, 9 Technicians, 12 Breathless). That split is useful for comparing rungs. It is not Tilly's durable inequality or Payne's pyramid, in which a small group holds most of the resource~\cite{payne2017broken,tilly1998durable}. An even three-cell design can understate how rare advantage is and how crowded the bottom is. Replications should unbalance both $n$ and resource shares.

Same-status focus groups made it easier to speak and also made ``we'' easier to say~\cite{Kitzinger1995}. Theme 3's belonging talk---stratum ranking hopes and same-floor camps (P3014, P3005, Breathless, Unequal)---is therefore both a finding and a method effect. Mixed-status rooms might produce more silence, system justification, or defection, and would test whether identity holds when the other rungs are present. The equal-condition groups remain a control: labels without gates did not create that ``we.''

The instruments are brief, and the traces are not independent. PRD, PBJW, DOSPERT, PANAS, and the allocation item cannot exhaust twenty minutes of situated play. Video-coded risk opportunities share players and sessions, so opportunity-level tests overstate precision. The allocation null is equally bounded: we cannot say inequality never moves redistribution, only that this hypothetical fairness task did not. In-game transfer logs, or a redistribution mechanic inside the world, would test a different claim.

Although the final sample exceeded the minimum $n$ estimated for the planned Condition $\times$ Time mixed-design analysis, status-specific tests within each condition used small cells. Those results should be read as status-specific patterns in this experimental context, and checked with larger samples before being treated as stable effect sizes.
