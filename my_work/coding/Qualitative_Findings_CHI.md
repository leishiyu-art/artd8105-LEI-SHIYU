# 5.2 Qualitative Findings

Same-status focus groups were conducted after each session. We analyzed transcripts using codebook thematic analysis aligned with the study RQs (perception of inequality; subsequent behavior). Four themes structure this section: (1) access as the primary cue for perceiving inequality; (2) status-differentiated risk orientation under inequality, supported by interviews and in-game observation; (3) stronger status identity and in-group collaboration under inequality than under equality; (4) risk-taking in both conditions, directed at different goals. Interview excerpts are tagged by participant ID, assigned status, and condition. Observational patterns in Theme 2 refer to video coding reported with the quantitative results (Figure X, map density; Figure Y, coded risk events).

### Theme 1: Access was the most direct cue for perceiving structural inequality

The unequal condition stacked five designed inequalities: spatial/vertical layout, resources, access, avatar appearance, and NPC/social capital. Across Architect, Technician, and Breathless groups, access—whether a portal could be used, and whether movement was one-way—was the cue players used to infer that they occupied different structural positions. Combat attributes were identical across statuses. Appearance and titles were often misread as faction markers rather than rank. Players therefore located inequality in who could move where, not in who hit harder.

Breathless players treated failed upward travel as evidence: “The only thing I felt was unfair was not being able to teleport to the upper level; finding out I couldn’t go was really disappointing” (P10, Breathless, Unequal). Restriction could be discovered through attempted use: “I wanted to ambush at the portal; I stood on it and nothing happened, and that’s when I knew this floor couldn’t climb up” (P3014, Breathless, Unequal). Access also recoded the portal: “Our identity can’t go up. For proletarians the portal is a threat, not an exchange route, so we hid” (P3010, Breathless, Unequal).

Architects described the same mechanic from the privileged side: “We can go down and take their crystals, and we can store crystals on the top floor” (P3008, Architect, Unequal). A blocked return trip made hierarchy legible after collection: “Walking a loop downstairs there were only ten or twenty; you couldn’t sit the portal back up, so you knew there was a difference” (P1004, Architect, Unequal). Technicians read posted rules: “The sign said proletarians can’t go up; blue can go to the middle and the lower floor” (P0005, Technician, Unequal).

Equal-condition groups did not have this one-way constraint. When the moderator asked what happened when cross-floor movement opened, an Equal Breathless player asked back, “What is cross-floor movement?” (P4001, Breathless, Equal). After the portal was explained, the same player treated it as a search and dodge tool, not a rank gate: “Go look for crystals elsewhere. If you see someone you can teleport over and back to hide” (P4001, Breathless, Equal). Status labels lacked structural meaning. Technicians said they “didn’t really have a concept of [their] own tier” and that “everyone seemed the same, same goal, not that different” (P2016; P2011, Technician, Equal). Architects reported color without privilege: “I seemed to be yellow, the people I met were blue, but ability and gameplay felt the same” (P4008, Architect, Equal). Equal Breathless players likewise read appearance as uniform: “Most people were blue. There wasn’t much difference. You couldn’t tell from appearance or the letters who hit you” (P4001, Breathless, Equal); “They all looked the same. There didn’t seem to be any difference” (P4005, Breathless, Equal). Asked who they compared themselves with, P4001 said there were not really same-tier people: “It was just a ranking list” (P4001, Breathless, Equal). This pattern is consistent with personal relative deprivation differentiating by status only in the unequal condition (Section 5.1).

### Theme 2: Under inequality, risk orientation split by status

Interviews and video observation jointly indicate that a perceived structural position did not produce a uniform “lower status, more risk” pattern. Technicians described two-sided exposure and counter-attack. Architects described protecting accumulated resources. Breathless players both avoided combat and approached it, in a layout that afforded denser resource encounters. Equal-condition risk-taking was not organized by status (Theme 4). Survey risk scores and coded event rates appear in Section 5.1; this subsection specifies mechanisms.

Technicians: two-sided exposure and counter-attack. Middle-status players could be engaged by Architects moving down, yet could not retreat to a protected top floor. After reading access rules, they reoriented from search to intercept: “Once I saw the restriction, I decided to attack anyone coming down from above” (P0005, Technician, Unequal); “When people from above appeared on the periphery instead of at the center door, I chased and hit them there” (P0005, Technician, Unequal). The shift could be explicit: “After I found I could attack and pick up others’ items, I stopped searching and started attacking other players” (P1003, Technician, Unequal). Breathless players named the same squeeze: “The top floor is most comfortable—you can hit and then escape up; the middle is worst, attacked from both sides” (P3014, Breathless, Unequal).

Architects: risk avoidance to protect prior accumulation. High-status players treated downward movement as optional and costly. Typical sequences were: clear the top layer, hide from others, store items upstairs, then descend only when opponents were far from the portal. “I first collected most of the crystals on the top floor, hid when I saw people, and followed down after they went down” (P1016, Architect, Unequal). Spatial advantage enabled delayed engagement: “From the middle you can see fighting below; I waited until they were far from the teleport point, then went down to ambush” (P1016, Architect, Unequal). Storage lowered the cost of remaining upstairs and raised the cost of a failed descent: “This floor has two ender chests, so we can store things upstairs first and then go down” (P3008, Architect, Unequal). Players stated the trade-off: staying and hiding could still yield a mid ranking, whereas going down risked losing everything (P1016, Architect, Unequal).

Breathless: avoidance and approach in a denser encounter space. Low-status interviews in the unequal condition did not converge on one strategy. Some withdrew after contact with upper floors: “When they came down we were helpless; after I saved crystals I hid on the second floor and wouldn’t even show my face” (P3014, Breathless, Unequal); “I went from searching boldly, to creeping downstairs once I knew you could die, to hiding even more conservatively on the second floor once I knew you could steal” (P3005, Breathless, Unequal). Others switched into retaliation or open combat: “After collecting a lot I wanted to hide; after being killed and respawning I didn’t want to search anymore, I only wanted revenge” (Breathless, Unequal); “After we fought someone with a teammate and got crystals, I attacked whoever I saw” (P3014, Breathless, Unequal).

Video observation specifies opportunity structure rather than a single preference. Resource nodes were denser on the lower floor (Figure X). Approaching a node was simultaneously collection and encounter; successful contact could replace scarce local stock. Architects’ early accumulation on a sparser top floor raised the cost of the same contact. We interpret Breathless approach behavior, when it occurred, as use of that denser layout, not as uniformly higher self-reported risk propensity.

Equal-condition Breathless groups showed a similar search–fight–hide sequence, but without a vertical position to defend or seize. “At first there was only one goal: find crystals. Then two: find crystals and fight. In the end three: find crystals, fight, and hide” (P4001, Breathless, Equal). “At first I searched openly. Later I searched more timidly. When I’d found enough I hid” (P4001, Breathless, Equal). “At first I chased and hit. Then I saw a lot of people die, so I hid. Someone came after me and I kept hiding” (P4004, Breathless, Equal). “At the beginning I just searched. After I saw people die I searched while hiding from people” (P4005, Breathless, Equal). Coded risk events were higher overall in the equal condition than in the unequal condition, and were not stratified by status (Figure Y; Theme 4).

### Theme 3: Inequality made status identity available, and players sought in-group collaboration; equality did not

Once access and appearance made a shared floor recognizable, unequal-condition players used collective language (“us,” “our side,” “same stratum”) and sought same-status help or giving. Equal-condition titles did not map onto privileges. Players could ignore clothing in combat. Cooperation that did occur served logistics or a temporary anti-aggression pact, rather than status identification.

Breathless players described grouping as an emergent reading of look and floor: “Appearance and different floors unnaturally split people into camps, alliances and counter-alliances” (P3005, Breathless, Unequal). Visual fit licensed cooperation and targeting: “The lower-floor look matches the identity, high recognition; it implies teammates cooperate and opponents can be attacked” (P3014, Breathless, Unequal). Ranking talk stayed inside the stratum: “I don’t want to compare with teammates; I just hope our whole stratum ranks high” (P3014, Breathless, Unequal). Kill-feed events were read as team news: “Seeing in the message bar that our side beat the blue side was encouraging” (P3010, Breathless, Unequal).

Giving followed perceived in-groups, including at the top. A Technician who believed the session was team-based “gave others both what I found in chests and what I robbed” (P0005, Technician, Unequal). Architects redistributed inside the high-status group: “I gave everything I found to the other architects and then went down to look; I ranked last” (P3008, Architect, Unequal); a recipient reported that “teammates threw crystals to me when I came down” (P3003, Architect, Unequal). A Breathless player reported an unexpected transfer from another player (Breathless, Unequal).

Equal-condition talk lacked this identity lock. “When I killed people I didn’t care what they wore, I just wanted crystals” (P2007, Architect, Equal). “I had no idea about factions or not” (P4002, Architect, Equal). Equal Breathless players could not map hits onto identities: “You couldn’t tell from appearance or the letters who hit you… I didn’t even remember it was number 4 who hit me first” (P4001, Breathless, Equal). One player proposed on public chat that they “join up, take out the person who was hitting people first, then split up to search” (P4001, Breathless, Equal). That proposal was a temporary coalition against plunder, not a same-stratum group. The other two Breathless players did not join it: one “couldn’t beat [P4001], so I ran” (P4004, Breathless, Equal); another “hid as soon as I saw people” and used the portal to shake a follower (P4005, Breathless, Equal). Task-based cooperation remained possible among Technicians: “The only time we actually cooperated was going through the portal together” (P2011, Technician, Equal). Collaboration under inequality therefore tracked recognizable structural difference, not the Breathless label alone.

### Theme 4: Both conditions involved risk-taking; the goal of that risk-taking differed

Combat and plunder were common in both conditions. Interviews distinguished what the risky act was for.

In the unequal condition, attacks followed the vertical structure: Architects descended to take from lower floors; Technicians intercepted those descents; Breathless players hid from, ambushed at, or retaliated against the portal. Hostility was timed to structural contact: “After we finished local resources, the upper floors came to plunder, and only then we were full of hostility toward the two floors above” (P3010, Breathless, Unequal). Downward presence also forced trajectory change: “People from above came down in better condition, so we could only hide; that changed the circling path I had been using to find crystals” (P3005, Breathless, Unequal). Risk-taking responded to rank and circulation: protecting, seizing, or blocking a position in the stacked world.

In the equal condition, there was no privileged circulation to contest. The turning point was discovering that killing dropped crystals. Equal Breathless players described that discovery as delayed and incomplete: “By default I thought you couldn’t fight. After I got hit I understood. I didn’t even know whether crystals would drop until I saw the result” (P4001, Breathless, Equal); “I hit someone twice and thought there was no damage. Then I saw on public chat that someone died, and realized you could attack people” (P4005, Breathless, Equal). Another player started hitting because search had failed: “I couldn’t find crystals, so when I saw someone I hit them” (P4004, Breathless, Equal). Other equal-status groups reported the same switch: “I happened to find that killing someone got me crystals, so later I started attacking people” (P2004, Breathless, Equal); “Once everyone knew you could kill, kill frequency clearly went up” (P2011, Technician, Equal). Fairness complaints targeted that discovery, not blocked travel: “At first picking things up felt fair; after I knew you could chase people and kill them it felt unfair—the rules weren’t explained thoroughly” (P4002, Architect, Equal); “It felt unfair. Some people just hit at random. If you didn’t know, you’d get backstabbed” (P4001, Breathless, Equal).

Equal-condition players still took risks, but toward plunder as a revealed rule rather than toward a status position. That distinction helps explain why combat appeared in both conditions while status-differentiated risk scores did not appear under equality (Section 5.1).
